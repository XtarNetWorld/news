Voyager 1 Just Crossed a Line No Human-Made Object Ever Has

NASA calculates Voyager 1 reached one light-day from Earth — about 16.1 billion miles — at 5:16am EST on Wednesday 18 November 2026. It is the first human-made object ever to get that far from home, and its radio signals now take a full day to reach us.

Somewhere out in the dark, further from Earth than anything humans have ever built, a machine the size of a small car is still doing its job. On Wednesday, Voyager 1 crossed a line measured not in miles but in time itself: one light-day. A message sent to it today arrives tomorrow.

What one light-day actually means

A light-day is a distance, not a time: how far light travels in 24 hours. For Voyager 1, that is about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion km) — more than four times the distance from the Sun to Pluto. NASA calculates the probe crossed that line at 5:16am EST on Wednesday 18 November (3:46pm IST).

From now on, every message to Voyager 1 travels at the speed of light and still needs a full day to get there. A command sent today, and the probe’s reply, is a 48-hour round trip. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory has scheduled an event for Thursday 19 November to discuss the milestone — it takes about a day for a signal confirming the crossing to reach Earth at all.

The distance wobbles a little through the year as Earth orbits the Sun, so 18 November is the first time the line was crossed. Voyager 1 is the first human-built object of any kind to reach it.

A 5-year mission, 49 years on

Voyager 1 launched from Cape Canaveral on 5 September 1977, sixteen days after its identical twin Voyager 2, on a five-year mission to fly past Jupiter and Saturn. It did that, and then it just kept going — past the planets, and in August 2012 across the heliopause, the edge of the Sun’s magnetic bubble, into interstellar space.

It has held the record as the most distant human-made object since passing Pioneer 10 in 1998. Only one antenna on Earth is powerful enough to send it commands: Deep Space Station 43, a 70-metre dish at the Canberra Deep Space Communication Complex in Australia.

It no longer works as it did in its prime. Eight of its original ten instruments are not operating, and the output from its plutonium-238 power source degrades a little each year. NASA engineers have kept it alive with backup thrusters and rerouted data — and NASA hopes it will keep communicating into the early 2030s, before its power finally drops below the minimum needed to run anything at all.

What it is carrying, and where it is going

Both Voyagers carry a gold-plated copper disc — the Golden Record — engraved with greetings, music and pictures representing life on Earth, in case anything ever finds it. Voyager 1 is heading in the direction of Gliese 445, a star more than 17 light-years away in the constellation Ursa Minor. If it keeps going, it could pass within 1.7 light-years of that star — in about 40,000 years.

To mark the moment, NASA asked the public to send a light signal into space after sunset on Tuesday 17 November, as part of its ‘Shine Bright for Voyager Light Day’ campaign. Voyager 2, meanwhile, is about nine years behind: it is expected to reach one light-day in November 2035.
