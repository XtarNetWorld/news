Signal Just Turned Off Screenshots — Because Your Phone's AI Was Reading Your Chats

Signal announced on 20 November 2026 that it will block screenshots inside the app, so the AI assistants living inside your phone — Siri, Gemini — can no longer read your private chats off the screen. It is a drastic answer to a problem encryption alone cannot solve, and Signal itself admits it is not enough.

Your messages are end-to-end encrypted. Nobody can read them in transit — not Signal, not your network provider, not a hacker listening in. And yet, right now, something else can read them: the AI assistant living inside your own phone. It does not need to break your encryption. It just looks at your screen. On 20 November 2026, Signal decided to take the screen away from it.

The lock on your screen

Signal announced on 20 November 2026 that it will restrict screenshots inside the app. The reason is not a bug or a leak. It is the way modern phones now work: operating systems have built AI agents — Apple’s Siri AI, Google’s Gemini — deep into the phone itself, and those agents can read what is on your screen. Your chats are encrypted end to end, but encryption protects the message on its journey. Once the message is displayed on your screen, the AI living in your phone can simply look at it.

Signal’s answer is to take the screen away from the agents. Screenshots inside Signal will be blocked, so there is nothing for an on-device AI to capture, copy or read. It is a blunt instrument, and Signal knows it — but in its view, the alternative is worse: a private messenger whose messages are quietly readable by the software that shares your phone.

How it works on iPhone

On iOS 27, screenshots inside Signal are blocked by default. There is also a new setting called “Block Siri AI”, aimed squarely at Apple’s assistant. Users can switch the protection off in settings if they want to — Signal does not force it — but the app warns that switching it off exposes their chats to the agents. And not only their own chats: the warning covers their contacts’ messages too, because a conversation belongs to both sides of it.

That last point is the heart of Signal’s argument. Privacy in a messenger is not only about your phone. When your AI assistant reads a chat on your screen, it is also reading the words of the person who wrote to you — someone who never agreed to share their messages with your phone’s AI.

How it works on Android

On Android, Signal is using a feature called Screen Security. Instead of a blanket block, it switches on when the app detects an AI assistant trying to read the screen. The idea is the same — keep the screen out of the agent’s reach — but the trigger is the attempt itself, so the protection appears exactly when it is needed.

The hole Signal cannot plug

Signal is unusually open about what this fix does not do. First, it does not work on macOS. Signal says it simply lacks the tools there to block the same access, so Mac users have to exclude Signal from Siri themselves. Second, blocking screenshots does not stop an agent reaching data that is already on the device through the filesystem. A screenshot is only one way in; if the data sits on the phone, an agent with the right access can still find it another way.

That is why Signal is not calling this a solution. It is calling it a holding action — a way to close the most obvious door while it pushes the companies that own the operating systems to build proper ones.

Why Signal calls it an existential threat

Signal’s language is unusually strong for a product announcement. It calls deep OS-agent integration an “existential threat” to its privacy guarantee — the promise that only the people in a conversation can read it. The threat is not a single feature. It is the direction of travel: AI assistants are being woven into every layer of the phone, from the keyboard to the notifications to the screen itself, and each layer is another place a private message can be read by software its sender never chose.

This is not the first time Signal has done this. Last year it took the same step on Windows 11, blocking screenshots to stop Microsoft’s Recall feature from capturing chats. The pattern is now clear: wherever an operating system builds a tool that can watch the screen, Signal will try to put its chats behind a wall.

What Signal wants next

Signal is asking Apple, Google and Microsoft for documented opt-outs for apps — formal ways for an app to say no to screen reading, to notification access and to filesystem access. The argument is that privacy should not depend on each app inventing its own workaround for each new OS feature. If the platforms offered a clear “do not read this app” switch, apps like Signal would not need to block screenshots at all.

There is also an uncomfortable piece of context this week. Reuters reported earlier in the week that Meta’s Muse AI agent pulled Signal messages on Mac and Android via accessibility permissions. That is a reported claim attributed to Reuters via The Times of India — not a finding by Signal — but it is the kind of report that explains why Signal is moving now, and why it is moving loudly.

For users, the change is simple: your screenshots inside Signal will stop working, on purpose. For the industry, the message is bigger: the phone in your pocket is no longer just your phone. It has a second reader now, and Signal has decided that reader is not welcome.

Quick Answers
Why is Signal blocking screenshots? +

Signal says OS-level AI agents such as Apple’s Siri AI and Google’s Gemini can read private chats straight off the screen, bypassing end-to-end encryption. Blocking screenshots stops those agents capturing what is displayed inside Signal.

Can I turn the screenshot block off? +

Yes, users can switch it off in Signal’s settings. But Signal warns that doing so exposes their chats — and their contacts’ messages — to the AI agents on the device.

Does the screenshot block work on Mac? +

No. Signal says it lacks the tools to block the same access on macOS, so Mac users must exclude Signal from Siri themselves.
