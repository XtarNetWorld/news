Sam Altman on Raj Shamani's Podcast: India Is ChatGPT's Biggest Market

In a 68-minute conversation released on 3 November 2026, OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman tells Raj Shamani why India, not the United States, now has the most ChatGPT users, how two huge bets shaped OpenAI, and why he worries most about AI power concentrating in too few hands.

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 5, 2026 • 14 min read • Tech
01 India

Altman says India is OpenAI’s biggest market by ChatGPT users, ahead of the US, and growing very quickly.

02 The bets

Training GPT-4 finished around August 2022; OpenAI then bet on far more computing power, and launched ChatGPT early.

03 The warning

Two risks: loss of control to AI, and one person, company or country becoming too powerful through AI.

04 The advice

Listen to less advice, build something people want, and do not wait until version one feels perfect.

The most quoted line from Sam Altman's appearance on Raj Shamani's podcast is also the simplest: India, Altman says, now has more ChatGPT users than the United States. It is a sentence that reframes a familiar story. For years, the centre of the AI conversation has been Silicon Valley. In this 68-minute episode of Figuring Out, released on 3 November 2026, Altman describes a different centre of gravity — Indian users and builders adopting AI tools faster, with more optimism, and for more practical purposes than many Americans. Around that headline, the conversation becomes something else too: a founder explaining, in order, how OpenAI bet its future twice, why it launched a product it did not think was ready, and what keeps him up at night about superintelligence. This article follows that conversation chapter by chapter, in the order Altman and Shamani discussed it, and separates what Altman is reported to have said from what is only paraphrase or background. It is not a transcript: no full transcript of the episode was available, and short quoted lines here come from reputable press recaps of the episode, which quote Altman.

Who these people are, in plain terms

Sam Altman is the chief executive of OpenAI, the San Francisco company behind ChatGPT. Born in Chicago in 1985 and raised near St. Louis, he left Stanford after two years to co-found Loopt, a location-sharing app later sold to Green Dot, then led the startup accelerator Y Combinator as its president from 2014 to 2019. He co-founded OpenAI in December 2015 and oversaw the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022, which turned a research lab into a household name. He was briefly removed by OpenAI’s board in November 2023 and reinstated days later after staff and investor pressure — background that matters when the podcast turns to leadership and departures.

Raj Shamani is an Indian entrepreneur, content creator and investor, and host of Figuring Out, a long-form interview podcast described by the show as a place for candid conversations with leading figures in India and beyond. OpenAI is the company behind ChatGPT, a conversational AI assistant, and the GPT family of large language models — systems trained on vast amounts of text that predict and generate language, and that have become the foundation for a wave of AI products.

Two terms run through the whole episode and are worth defining before anything else. Artificial general intelligence, or AGI, means AI that can reason and perform intellectual tasks at or above human level across many domains, not just one narrow task. Superintelligence means AI far beyond human capability. Altman is not talking only about better chatbots. He is talking about how to steer systems that may soon improve themselves.

India: the headline, and why Altman thinks Indians are optimistic

Shamani asks whether India is bigger than the United States for ChatGPT. Altman replies, as reported by multiple outlets, “Yeah. More ChatGPT is just India than the US” and “India is our biggest market in the world by ChatGPT users and growing very quickly.” The qualifier matters: biggest by users, not necessarily by revenue. The episode does not, in the recaps found, claim India is OpenAI’s biggest market by money, and this article does not make that claim either.

Altman credits what he calls “incredible builder energy” — Indians using the tools to build companies and solve practical problems, not only to ask questions. His example, as reported, is an Indian school student with no fundraising experience who used OpenAI’s tools to organise a complete school fundraising effort, described as “the full stack of it”. The point is not the one student. It is the pattern Altman says he sees: people with little formal experience using AI to do work that once needed a team, a budget or an expert.

On why Indians seem more optimistic about AI than Americans, Altman points to economics. As reported, he says: “One thing that many people have observed is that countries that are developing faster, where the economy is growing faster and there’s like more of a need for products and services and like, you know, there’s just huge undersupply, are more welcoming of AI than more developed economies.” In other words, where demand is unmet, a tool that helps people produce more feels like opportunity. Where markets are already saturated, the same tool can feel like a threat. He also says Americans are, on expressed preference, “unusually negative on AI relative to the rest of the world” and India “maybe unusually positive”, with a random person in the US “slightly pessimistic” about AI while people in India are “very optimistic”.

Leadership: why pushback matters more than agreement

The next chapter turns to leadership. Per the episode description, Altman discusses how he avoids surrounding himself with yes-men and why pushback matters for long-term decisions. Secondary analysis of the episode summarises his rule as building a culture that rewards pushback so a leader does not drown in agreement, and staying consistent with a position until there is a reason to change one’s mind. The exact wording in this segment was not captured in the reputable recaps found, so this article presents it as paraphrase, not as a quote.

The idea is easy to state and hard to practise, especially for a chief executive whose company has become one of the most watched in the world. Agreement feels efficient. It also removes the friction that catches bad ideas early. Altman’s argument, as paraphrased, is that long-term decisions — the kind OpenAI has to make about computing, safety and product timing — get worse, not better, when everyone in the room already agrees with the leader.

Scaling: the idea that changed AI

Altman then explains the surprising properties of scale: neural networks kept getting better as they were scaled up, a pattern he says also shows up in companies, cities and individual careers, but was clearest in AI. When OpenAI started, he says, it was “extremely low status” and almost nobody took the effort seriously; few groups were willing to say openly that they wanted to build AGI.

This is the intellectual foundation for everything that follows. If bigger models, more data and more computing power keep producing qualitative jumps — not just slightly better answers, but new capabilities — then the rational strategy is to bet on scale, even before the results are public. If scale stops working, the strategy fails. The rest of the episode is, in large part, the story of OpenAI acting on the first belief.

The GPT-4 bet: everything, before the world had seen it

OpenAI finished training GPT-4 around August 2022, months before its public release in March 2023. Seeing what the model could do, Altman says, the company bet everything on buying far more computing power — before the wider industry had seen GPT-4. The alternative, he says, was to invest less and move at a slower pace.

Secondary analysis renders his framing as “50% certainty and 50% delusion” — the bet was made seven or eight months before GPT-4’s release, on conviction about scale rather than proof. That phrase is presented here as secondary analysis, not as a verified verbatim quote from the episode. What is clear from the episode description and recaps is the shape of the decision: a near-existential capital bet taken on an internal result the world had not yet seen, in a company that could have chosen the slower, safer path.

The following chapter, on contrarian ideas and moonshots, extends the same logic to company-building in general. Per the episode description, Altman explains how he finds non-consensus bets — ideas most people dismiss — and how he separates genuine conviction from wishful thinking. Secondary analysis summarises his rule as picking something so valuable that the expected value is good even if it is unlikely to work. No verbatim quote from this segment was found in reputable recaps, and none is invented here.

Launching ChatGPT early: embarrassed by version one

Many people inside OpenAI thought it was too early to release ChatGPT because the product was not good enough. Altman overruled them, drawing on his Y Combinator experience: releasing an early version lets a company gather user feedback and improve, rather than waiting until it feels fully developed. The Y Combinator formulation, as reported in secondary analysis, is: if you are not a little embarrassed by your first version, you waited too long.

Internal retention at launch was reportedly only about 5 percent — but the small cohort that stayed used it heavily, which Altman read as signal. That detail matters because it reframes ChatGPT’s November 2022 debut. It was not launched as a polished product. It was launched as a research preview, and the people who stayed with an imperfect tool told OpenAI more about what mattered than a longer internal polish would have.

Superintelligence: two ways it goes wrong, and pluralism as the answer

Altman names two ways AI could go badly wrong, as reported: “I think there are two kind of cartoonish ways this goes really wrong. One is that there’s a loss of control to AI… And the other is one person or company or country gets way too powerful relative to everybody else. I think that’s very unstable.”

His answer is pluralism: “I believe in pluralism. I don’t think only us having it is good either” and “I also have this dislike of too intense power concentration.” Multiple competing AI efforts, following different approaches, are preferable to one entity controlling the technology. He also concedes the underlying criticism: Americans’ scepticism of the tech industry’s enormous power is “probably a good thing in some ways”, though it feels to him like it has “gone a little far”, and concerns about concentration are “not misplaced”.

On OpenAI’s own edge, secondary analysis says he points not to a single model but to the full stack — chips to data centres to models — plus the most users and a brand people want to trust. On the hardest current problem, he reportedly calls pacing progress in what he terms the recursive self-improvement era — a period of self-improving systems — the most uncomfortable part of his job, raising questions of governance, security, monitoring, alignment, and how smart a system should be relative to a person. Those last points are paraphrase from secondary analysis, not verified quotes.

Departures, research culture and life before OpenAI

The episode addresses why senior researchers have left OpenAI to found rival labs, and what research culture means at high stakes. This article deliberately does not name individual departures from this segment, because the framing found in secondary analysis was second-hand — one analysis citing another recap — and was not verified against the episode or a reputable news recap. Readers who want names should check the video at the 41:27 chapter rather than rely on social-media summaries.

What can be said safely is background, not episode claim: Altman’s pre-OpenAI career was Loopt, then Y Combinator, where he was president from 2014 to 2019. The episode’s chapter on life before OpenAI, startup moats and founder success covers those years and the idea of a moat — a durable competitive advantage — but no reputable recap found gives Altman’s specific moat claims from this episode, so this article does not pad that section with generic advice.

Founder advice: listen to less advice

Asked about the worst advice he received as a founder, Altman gives a meta-advice, as reported: “I would say that my meta advice to founders is listen to less advice.” Entrepreneurs cannot become successful simply by repeatedly consuming business advice or startup podcasts; they must learn by building products and solving problems themselves, since much of the advice a founder needs depends on the specific circumstances of the business.

He endorses Y Combinator’s core principle — build something people want — and, per the episode description, argues that ambition can be taught and that this is the best time in history to build. That last claim fits the India theme: tiny teams can now do work that once needed large teams. The episode’s exact team-size formulation was not verified in reputable recaps, so no specific team-size claim is attributed to Altman here.

The following chapter, on career growth, compounding and questioning one’s beliefs, is described in the episode listing as covering small improvements accumulating over time and the discipline of questioning one’s own views. No verbatim quote from that segment was found in reputable recaps.

What he wants to build next, and what it has cost him

Asked what technology he wants to develop next, Altman says AI systems that accelerate scientific research: helping researchers make discoveries in physics, develop new medical treatments and find new energy sources. He adds that recent advances in mathematics have made him more optimistic that AI can contribute to scientific breakthroughs sooner than expected. This is the episode’s forward-looking close: superintelligence justified not by chatbots, but by faster science.

The personal coda is quieter. Altman says his life has become more structured since OpenAI, with less freedom for spontaneous plans. He misses Saturday afternoons in coffee shops, working on his laptop, checking emails and browsing the internet — something he says he has not done for years — because security personnel now accompany him, making anonymity and freedom difficult.

In the same reflective register, he addresses his unusual position as OpenAI’s chief executive without owning equity, as reported: “I think not taking, not having equity in OpenAI has been a mistake. I did it for what I thought were good reasons. I think it’s just been hugely confusing to people.” He compares building OpenAI to an artist dedicating years to a project or an athlete practising for countless hours without necessarily making a fortune from it — among the most interesting and important things he could work on, even without financial ownership. Reputable recaps do not give a timestamp for the equity remarks, so this article does not assign them to a chapter.

What this episode does not claim about jobs

Readers may have seen social-media summaries claiming Altman said, in this podcast, that AI will not steal jobs, or that one person can now build what a team of ten used to, or giving a specific timeline for AGI. Those formulations were not found in the episode description or in reputable recaps, and they are not attributed to this podcast here. Altman has made jobs-related remarks in many other interviews; conflating those with this episode would be an error.

What the episode does say, in substance, is about leverage: AI as a builder’s tool, Indians using it to start businesses and complete work that once needed experience or teams, and the episode description’s claim that this is the best time in history to build. That is the episode’s jobs message — leverage for individuals and small teams — not a jobs forecast, and not a timeline.

That restraint is part of why the episode is worth listening to in full, rather than through clips. Clips give you the India line, the equity line and the advice line. The full 68 minutes give you the reasoning that connects them: scale as a bet, early release as a method, pluralism as a safeguard, and science as the reason the bet might be worth making at all.
