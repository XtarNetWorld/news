Intuit Just Sold Mailchimp for $3.68 Billion — After Paying $12 Billion for It

Five years ago Mailchimp was Intuit's biggest deal ever. Now it's gone for less than a third of the price — to a company famous for buying faded tech brands. What went wrong?

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 21, 2026 • 7 min read • Startups

Every big acquisition comes with a story the buyer tells on the day — about synergy, about vision, about the future. Five years later, the sale price tells a different story. Intuit bought Mailchimp in 2021 for $12 billion, the biggest deal in its history. On 19 November 2026, it agreed to sell the email-marketing pioneer to Bending Spoons for about $3.68 billion — less than a third of what it paid. The numbers alone make this one of the starkest reversals in recent tech dealmaking. The question is not just how much value disappeared, but why a business that once looked like the future of small-business marketing ended up as a bargain for a buyer famous for picking up faded brands.

The deal, in plain numbers

Intuit said on 19 November 2026 that it had agreed to sell Mailchimp to Bending Spoons for about $3.68 billion. The structure is $3.31 billion in cash, plus $368 million that Intuit will reinvest by taking shares in Bending Spoons. The deal is expected to close in early 2027, subject to regulatory approvals — which means it is agreed, not yet finished, and the usual caveat applies: until regulators clear it and the money changes hands, it is a signed agreement rather than a completed sale.

The comparison that defines this story is the one with 2021. Intuit bought Mailchimp that year for $12 billion — its largest acquisition ever, a bet that email marketing belonged at the centre of its small-business empire alongside QuickBooks and TurboTax. Five years later, the sale price is roughly 31 percent of the purchase price. In plain terms: about $8.3 billion of the value Intuit paid for has gone.

That is not quite the same as saying Intuit “lost” $8.3 billion in cash — the accounting of a five-year ownership, including the revenue Mailchimp generated in between and the costs of running it, is more complicated than a single subtraction. But the market reads a sale price this far below a purchase price as a verdict, and Intuit’s own shares slumped on the news. The deal was announced alongside Intuit’s fiscal first-quarter 2027 results, for the quarter ended 31 October 2026, so investors got the sale and the earnings in the same breath — and reacted to both at once.

Who is Bending Spoons — and why it buys brands like this

Bending Spoons is an Italian tech holding company, and its name will be unfamiliar to many readers outside the deal pages. It is backed by a major Berkshire Hathaway investment, and it has built a reputation for acquiring what the Wall Street Journal has called “wayward” tech brands — products that were once prominent, still have users, but have lost their momentum inside a bigger company.

That reputation is the key to understanding the price. Bending Spoons does not buy companies at their peak; it buys them when the seller has decided they are no longer central, and it bets that focused attention can do what a sprawling parent could not. For Mailchimp, that pitch is straightforward: a business whose core product — email marketing for small businesses — still has millions of users, but which inside Intuit was one division among many, competing for attention with accounting software and tax products.

Intuit is keeping a financial link to the business it is selling: $368 million of the price comes back as shares in Bending Spoons. That is a small stake, but it signals something — Intuit is not walking away entirely. It is converting a business it no longer wants to run into a minority interest in the company that will run it instead.

What went wrong — or, what changed

When Intuit bought Mailchimp in 2021, the logic was easy to state. Small businesses already used QuickBooks to run their money; Mailchimp would let Intuit reach the same customers at the other end of their working day, when they were trying to find and keep their own customers. Email was the oldest and most reliable channel in digital marketing, and Mailchimp was its best-known name. The price — $12 billion — was the cost of buying the category leader outright.

Five years on, the email-marketing market has not stood still. Competition from newer marketing platforms, the rise of messaging and social channels, and the shift of small-business attention toward AI-driven tools have all eaten into the position Mailchimp once held almost unchallenged. At the same time, Intuit’s own strategy has moved: its recent story has been about AI across its products, and about the tax and accounting core that generates most of its revenue. A standalone email-marketing brand, however well known, fits that story less neatly than it did in 2021.

None of that means Mailchimp failed as a product — it means it stopped being the right asset for Intuit to own at the price Intuit paid. That distinction matters, because it is the difference between a business that is broken and a business that is simply worth less to one particular owner than to another. Bending Spoons is betting it is the latter.

What the sale says about the 2021 deal boom

Mailchimp is not the first pandemic-era acquisition to be unwound at a fraction of its price, and it will not be the last. In 2021, cheap money and surging digital adoption pushed buyers to pay peak prices for software businesses; five years later, with interest rates higher and growth harder to find, those prices look optimistic. Intuit’s $12 billion is the largest single example, but the pattern is familiar across the industry: buy at the top, discover the growth was slower than forecast, sell at a discount to a specialist buyer.

What makes this case stand out is the scale of the discount — nearly 70 percent — and the identity of the buyer. Bending Spoons is not a rival marketing company absorbing a competitor. It is a holding company that specialises in exactly this kind of deal, and its willingness to pay $3.68 billion is itself a signal: Mailchimp, at that price, is worth buying. The market’s judgement, in other words, is not that Mailchimp is worthless. It is that it was worth nothing like $12 billion.

What happens next

Three things will decide how this sale is remembered. The first is regulatory approval: the deal needs clearance before it can close in early 2027, and while nothing in the announcement suggests an obstacle, approvals are never guaranteed. The second is what Bending Spoons does first — whether it invests in the product, cuts costs, or changes pricing for Mailchimp’s small-business customers, who will feel the ownership change before anyone else. The third is Intuit itself: what it does with $3.31 billion in cash, and whether investors judge the sale as a clean break from a bad bet or as an admission that the 2021 strategy failed.

For Mailchimp’s users, the honest answer for now is that nothing changes on day one. The deal has not closed, and Bending Spoons has not announced product plans. For the rest of the tech industry, the lesson is simpler, and it is written in two numbers: $12 billion in, $3.68 billion out. That is what five years, a changed market and a different owner are worth.
