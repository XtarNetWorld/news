51 Indian Startups Worth ₹3.3 Lakh Crore Are One Step From Unicorn Status

The Hurun India Gazelle Index 2026 lists 51 startups valued between $500 million and $1 billion that could become unicorns within three years. Eight of them are building AI, clean energy, rockets and drones.

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 3, 2026 • 6 min read • Startups
01 The List

51 Gazelles, up from 47 in 2025 and 32 in 2021, with a combined valuation of about ₹3.3 lakh crore and FY2025 revenue of ₹33,233 crore.

02 The Geography

Bengaluru leads with 17 Gazelles, followed by the National Capital Region with 11 and Mumbai with 8.

03 The Frontier Eight

Fifty-one Indian startups are now worth, together, about ₹3.3 lakh crore — and none of them is yet a unicorn. That is the premise of the Hurun India Gazelle Index 2026, released on 3 November, which lists unlisted startups valued between $500 million and $1 billion that are considered likely to reach unicorn status within three years. It is a list of near-misses, in the best sense: companies that have already proved they can build and sell at scale, and are now one funding round, one major contract, or one breakthrough away from the billion-dollar mark.

What a Gazelle is, and why 51 matters

A Gazelle, in Hurun's definition used for this index, is an unlisted startup founded in or after 2000, valued between $500 million and $1 billion, and likely to reach unicorn status — a valuation of $1 billion or more — within three years. The 2026 index, produced by ASK Private Wealth and Hurun India, lists 51 such companies, up from 47 in 2025 and 32 in 2021. Their combined valuation is about ₹3.3 lakh crore, and their FY2025 revenue was ₹33,233 crore across the 41 companies that reported it, with average growth of 50%.

Geography tells its own story. Bengaluru leads with 17 Gazelles, followed by the National Capital Region with 11 and Mumbai with 8. That distribution — roughly one in three Gazelles in Bengaluru — reflects where India's startup infrastructure is densest: talent, investors, customers and support services clustered in one city. It does not mean other cities are absent, but it does mean that a founder's location still shapes their odds, even in an age of remote work.

AI: digital employees, contact centres and connected apps

Three of the eight frontier Gazelles are in AI, and they illustrate three different ways AI is being sold to businesses. Ema AI, founded in 2023, develops AI-powered digital employees that can take on tasks across businesses, automating workflows, connecting with enterprise software and handling work across human resources, finance and customer service. It was founded in California and has an office in Bengaluru, and it reached Gazelle level within three years of founding — a sign of how quickly enterprise AI companies can scale when their product fits a clear business need.

Observe.AI, founded in 2017, develops AI tools for customer experience and contact centres. Its platform helps businesses automate customer interactions, assist human agents in real time and analyse conversations to improve customer service. It is headquartered in Redwood City, California, and its presence on an Indian Gazelle list reflects the deep India connection of many US-headquartered AI companies, through founders, teams and customers in India.

UnifyApps, founded in 2023, develops an enterprise AI platform that connects business applications, data and workflows, helping companies build AI agents and automate processes across different systems. It has offices in New York and in India, including Gurugram and Hyderabad, and like Ema AI, it reached Gazelle level within three years. Together, these three companies suggest that the most valuable AI startups in this cohort are not building chatbots for consumers, but tools that let businesses automate work they already do, more cheaply and more consistently.

CleanTech: capturing carbon, recycling batteries, making solar

Three frontier Gazelles are in CleanTech, a sector that has moved from environmental idealism to industrial necessity. Carbon Clean Solutions, founded in India in 2009 by Aniruddha Sharma and Prateek Bumb, develops technology to capture carbon dioxide from industrial processes, making carbon capture more efficient and accessible. Its age — 17 years old — is a reminder that CleanTech companies often take longer to reach scale than software companies, because they must build physical plants and prove them in industrial settings.

Lohum, founded in 2018 and based in Greater Noida, works on battery and critical-minerals recycling. As electric vehicles and energy storage grow, the minerals inside used batteries become a strategic resource, and recycling them is both an environmental and a supply-chain business. Rayzon Solar, founded in 2017, makes solar modules, riding India's solar expansion from the manufacturing side rather than the installation side.

Aerospace and defence: rockets from Chennai, drones from Noida

The most visually striking frontier Gazelles are the two in aerospace and defence. Agnikul Cosmos, founded in 2017 and based in Chennai, designs and manufactures launch vehicles for small satellites, including the Agnibaan vehicle with 3D-printed engines. Small-satellite launch is a market that did not exist at scale a decade ago, and Agnikul is one of a handful of Indian private companies trying to serve it, alongside ISRO's own programme and other startups.

Raphe mPhibr, founded in 2017 and based in Noida, designs and manufactures unmanned aerial vehicles — drones — for surveillance and logistics, along with customised systems. Drones have moved from hobbyist toys to serious tools for defence, agriculture, delivery and inspection, and Raphe mPhibr's presence on the Gazelle list reflects that shift. Together, Agnikul and Raphe mPhibr show that Indian startups are no longer confined to software and services. They are building hardware that flies, and that hardware is attracting the valuations to match.

Who graduated, and what the global funding picture adds

Seven Gazelles graduated to unicorn status in 2026: Twin Health, KreditBee, Cloudnine Hospitals, Dhan, La Renon, Neo Wealth Management and InVideo AI, the only AI graduate among them. Fifteen new entrants joined the index in 2026, including Ema AI and UnifyApps directly at Gazelle level within three years of founding. That churn — graduates leaving, new entrants arriving — is healthy. It means the index is not a static club, but a pipeline.

For context, the global funding picture reported on 3 November was led by non-Indian rounds, and this article keeps it to one paragraph deliberately, because a list of mostly Chinese and US rounds will not travel as well for Indian readers as the Hurun story. Moffett AI in Shenzhen announced a Series C+ approaching RMB 1 billion for AI inference chips on 3 November; Tutor Intelligence in the US raised $75 million in a Series B led by Andreessen Horowitz; Larridin raised $30 million in a Series A led by Pelion Ventures; and nPhase raised $30 million in a Series D led by Viking Global Investors — the last three announced on 2 November US time and reported on 3 November in India. The only Indian raise in that roundup was PowerUp Money, with $10 million for a wealth-management platform. That contrast — a global funding list with one Indian name, versus a Hurun list with 51 Indian companies — is itself a signal about where Indian startup news is most relevant to Indian readers.

What to watch next

The question for each Gazelle is the same: what gets it from $500 million to $1 billion? For Ema AI and UnifyApps, it may be enterprise contracts at scale. For Agnikul, it may be a successful commercial launch. For Raphe mPhibr, it may be defence or logistics contracts. For Lohum and Carbon Clean, it may be industrial scale and policy support for recycling and carbon capture. None of those paths is guaranteed, and some Gazelles will stall or fall back, as previous cohorts have.
