The New Startup Bet Is Not Another App: It Is Antennas, Thrusters and Factories

Two large rounds on 2 November 2026, for a satellite ground network and for hypersonic systems, point to the same shift: startup capital is moving from software alone into the physical systems underneath it.

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 3, 2026 • 6 min read • Startups
01 The Signal

On 2 November 2026, two large rounds landed in one day: one for satellite ground stations, one for hypersonic systems. The common thread was physical infrastructure, not another app.

02 The Lead

Northwood Space raised $375 million in Series C funding, at a reported $2 billion valuation, to build the shared ground network that connects satellites to Earth.

03 The Second Signal

Hypersonica raised €100 million in Series B funding to move its hypersonic prototype toward a deployable system, with production aimed at 2029.

04 The Catch

Hardware is slower and more capital-hungry than software. Factories, tests and government customers decide whether these bets pay off — not a launch post.

For a decade, a promising startup was usually described by its software. That description is starting to fail. On 2 November 2026, two large startup rounds went to companies that make things you can stand next to: antennas that talk to satellites, and a prototype built to fly faster than sound.

What matters is what the money is for. In both cases, investors are paying for factories, test programmes and networks, not only for code. Days earlier, India offered public funding to four space startups working on propulsion, timing and navigation hardware. Together, these are a readable signal about where startup capital is now willing to go.

What happened

Northwood Space, a satellite ground infrastructure company, said on 2 November 2026 that it had closed a $375 million Series C round, valuing the company at $2 billion. The round was co-led by Andreessen Horowitz and DFJ Growth, with participation from Alpine Space Ventures, Washington Harbour Partners, Stepstone Activate and In-Q-Tel. The company also has a separate HSBC Innovation Banking credit facility.

Northwood Space builds a shared, multi-orbit ground network for commercial, United States government and allied missions. Its Portal antenna is a phased array for command and control, capable of communicating with six satellites, while Prism is a high-throughput dish for one satellite at a time. The funding will help deliver on existing contracts and build out the network. The company employs 140 people and plans 400 more hires over three years.

It had raised a $100 million Series B round in January 2026, and earlier in the year it received a $49.8 million United States Space Force contract to support the Satellite Control Network, which provides telemetry, tracking and command for Department of Defense satellites.

On the same day, Hypersonica, a British-German defence startup based in Munich with a subsidiary in London, raised €100 million in Series B funding, co-led by Lightspeed Venture Partners and General Catalyst, with Plural and 201 Ventures participating. Founded in 2023, it had raised a €23.2 million Series A round in February 2026, taking reported total financing to about €125 million. Financial Times reporting put its valuation at around €700 million, although the company has not disclosed one itself.

Hypersonica aims to have its hypersonic deep-strike systems fully developed by 2029. Its HS-1 prototype was tested in February 2026, flying above Mach 6 over more than 300 kilometres, a first the company claims for a privately funded European defence company. The funding will increase testing, expand European operations and create hundreds of engineering and manufacturing jobs in the United Kingdom and Germany.

Two days earlier, India's IN-SPACe selected four companies for support under its Technology Adoption Fund, offering Rs 67.15 crore to develop indigenous space technologies. The four were Manastu Space, Inspecity Space Laboratories, VyomIC and MumbaiSemi, taking the fund's total to seven companies, after three were selected in June 2026. Projects will be monitored against defined milestones.

Why it matters

Start with the ground. A satellite is only useful if someone on Earth can talk to it and receive its data. As more satellites launch, that ground layer becomes a bottleneck: every operator either builds its own stations or rents time on someone else's. Northwood Space is betting that renting time on a shared network becomes the normal choice, as renting computing capacity did. That is a long build, but if it works, it becomes hard to replace.

Hypersonica points to a second motive: sovereignty. The company develops and controls its technology in Europe, within a wider effort to build long-range strike capability at home. The United Kingdom's Defence Investment Plan has allocated an initial £770 million over four years to the UK-German Deep Precision Strike programme. For founders, that changes the customer: the buyer is not only a venture fund, but also a state with a programme and a budget.

India's IN-SPACe selections show the same logic from the public side. The four projects cover propulsion, in-orbit servicing, precision timing and navigation chips — parts of a national space capability, not consumer products. Public funding here de-risks work private investors might find too slow or specialised to back alone.

The numbers, side by side

The three announcements are different kinds of money, but side by side they show how the same theme is being funded in three different ways.

DealAmountWhat it funds
Northwood Space, Series C$375 millionA shared satellite ground network, antenna production and hiring

Manastu Space was offered Rs 22.08 crore for an iodine-based hall-effect thruster, Inspecity Rs 21.18 crore for a refuellable six-degree-of-freedom propulsion system, VyomIC Rs 12.55 crore for stable, secure space clocks, and MumbaiSemi Rs 11.34 crore for a multi-channel NavIC and GPS receiver chip. Those four offers add up exactly to Rs 67.15 crore.

The trade-offs nobody skips

Hardware startups fail in ways software startups usually do not. A test can fail, a factory can slip, and a customer delay can leave a production line waiting. Northwood Space's plan to add 400 people over three years raises the cost of being wrong about timing. Hypersonica's 2029 target is a public deadline, and a prototype that flew more than 300 kilometres is still a long way from a deployable system.

Government customers cut both ways: they can offer large, multi-year programmes, but they can also change priorities and stretch timetables. Valuation is the other risk: Northwood Space's $2 billion valuation and the reported figure for Hypersonica are prices for future delivery, not measurements of current profit.

What happens next

The next twelve months will be more informative than the announcements. For Northwood Space, the question is execution: turning a Series C round into a network operators actually use. For Hypersonica, it is testing: moving from a prototype flight to a system with guidance and a credible path to production by 2029. For the IN-SPACe companies, it is milestones: steps that either unlock further support or reveal where the work is harder than expected.

The calm way to read 2 November 2026 is not as a boom or a bubble, but as a widening: startup capital is learning, again, to pay for things that must work in the physical world first.

Quick Answers
Why are startups raising money for antennas and missiles instead of software? +

Because the bottleneck has moved. Satellites need ground stations to talk to Earth, and governments want systems built at home. That work needs factories, antennas and test flights, which is why it attracts large, long-term rounds.

What does Northwood Space actually build? +

Northwood Space builds a shared ground network that connects satellites to Earth. Its Portal antenna is a phased array for command and control, and its Prism antenna is a high-throughput dish for payload data.

Is this just a one-day funding story? +

No. The two large rounds on 2 November 2026 followed a United States Space Force contract for Northwood Space and came days after India's IN-SPACe offered funding to four space startups, which points to a wider shift rather than a single news day.
