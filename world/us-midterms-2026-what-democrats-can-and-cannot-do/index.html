Democrats Won Everything — So Why Can’t They Just Pass Whatever They Want?

The Democrats took the House, the Senate and most governorships on 3 November. That is a real shift in power — but in Washington, winning the chambers is not the same as being able to pass whatever you want.

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 6, 2026 • 5 min read • World
01 Senate

Democrats hold at least 54 seats (projection, as of 5 Nov) — above the 51 needed for a majority.

02 House

Democrats reached 242 seats (projection, as of 5 Nov) — above the 218 needed for control; NBC projected they may end up with 259.

03 Governors

Democrats won most of the nation’s governorships for the first time since 2010.

04 The limit

Still short of the 60 Senate votes needed to beat a filibuster, and of the two-thirds majority needed in either chamber to override a veto.

On the night of 3 November, the headline was simple: the Democrats won. By the morning after, the picture was bigger than a single race. Media projections from AP, NBC, CNN and Nikkei Asia pointed the same way — control of both chambers of Congress, plus most governorships across the country. That is a genuine change in who sets the agenda in Washington. It is also not the end of the story. The new Congress does not take office until January, and final counts were still moving as votes were counted on 4–5 November. Even with both chambers, there are hard limits built into how American lawmaking works.

What actually happened

The election was held on 3 November 2026. In the Senate, Democrats gained 7 seats, according to Times Now, citing NBC and the Washington Post, and hold at least 54 seats — above the 51 needed for a majority. Two Senate races had not been called as of 5 November — Alaska and Mississippi — so that number could still move slightly, and counting continues there.

In the House, Democrats flipped 35 seats, according to NBC via Times Now, reaching 242 seats against the 218 needed for control. NBC projected they may end up with 259 seats. Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic House leader, is poised to become the first-ever Black Speaker, according to Al Jazeera.

The shift went beyond Washington. Democrats won most governorships for the first time since 2010, according to Times Now. In Wisconsin, they flipped both chambers, giving them full control of state government for the first time in almost two decades.

All of these numbers are projections as of 5 November, not final certified results.

The firsts that made history

Some results were historic in themselves. In Texas, James Talarico beat Ken Paxton, 59.2% versus 39.2% at 67% counted — the first Democrat to win statewide in Texas since 1994. In Kansas, Adam Hamilton became the first Democrat to represent Kansas in the US Senate in 94 years, according to Al Jazeera. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed won, becoming the first Muslim US senator, according to USA Today and Al Jazeera.

Other Senate flips: North Carolina (Roy Cooper beat Trump-backed Michael Whatley), South Carolina (Annie Andrews beat incumbent Darline Graham), Ohio (Sherrod Brown beat Jon Husted, returning after a two-year absence), Iowa (Josh Turek), and Maine (Troy Jackson beat five-term Susan Collins). In Georgia, Jon Ossoff was re-elected.

Among governors, Georgia elected Keisha Lance Bottoms — the first Black woman elected governor of a US state; Texas elected Gina Hinojosa — the first Latina governor of Texas; and Ohio elected Amy Acton — the first woman elected governor of Ohio. Democrats also flipped Iowa and Nevada, where Aaron Ford beat incumbent Lombardo.

What they can do now

Winning both chambers gives Democrats control of the agenda in the House and the Senate. They decide what gets debated, what gets voted on, and what does not reach the floor at all.

They also gain subpoena and investigative power. Voters named corruption a top concern, according to Times Now, and that power is one of the most direct tools a new majority holds. They can block President Trump’s legislative agenda, and gain leverage where the Senate matters on appointments.

In short, they can stop things, investigate things, and set the terms of debate.

What they still cannot do

Here is the part the headline leaves out. Winning a majority is not the same as being able to pass major legislation alone.

In the Senate, Democrats are still short of the 60 votes needed to beat a filibuster. That means a minority can block most bills from reaching a final vote, unless enough Republicans join them.

In neither chamber do Democrats have the two-thirds majority required to override a veto. President Trump has said he will use the veto aggressively, according to Times Now. So even if a bill passes both chambers, the president can stop it — and Congress would not have the votes to push it through anyway.

Put plainly: they cannot pass major legislation alone without Republican votes or the president’s signature.

Why voters moved — as reported

Why did voters move this way? We have reported polling and reaction, not proven cause. AP polling cited by Al Jazeera found the economy was the top issue for about 4 in 10 voters. Nikkei reported widespread discontent over Trump’s policies on inflation, tariffs, AI data centres and the Iran war. Trump himself said the Iran war played a role in Republican losses and that voters were probably unwilling to accept higher petrol prices. He said he will accept the results, according to the Al Jazeera live blog.

What to watch in January

The new Congress takes office in January. Come January, Trump will face a Democratic Congress, according to WAMC.

Watch three things. First, the final certified seat counts, which will settle the exact margins. Second, who leads the investigations, and what they look at first. Third, whether the new majority and the president find any ground where a bill can pass both chambers and survive a veto — or whether the next two years are defined mainly by blocking, investigating and preparing for the next election.

The Democrats won everything on the ballot in front of them. What happens next will depend on what they can do with a majority that is real, but not unlimited.

Quick Answers
How many Senate seats did the Democrats win? +

At least 54 seats, according to projections as of 5 November — above the 51 needed for a majority. Alaska and Mississippi were still uncalled, so counting continues and the number could still move.

Can Democrats now pass any law they want? +

No. They are short of the 60 Senate votes needed to beat a filibuster, and short of the two-thirds majority needed in either chamber to override a presidential veto.

When does the new Congress take office? +

In January. Until then, the outgoing Congress remains in place and final certified counts are still being settled.
