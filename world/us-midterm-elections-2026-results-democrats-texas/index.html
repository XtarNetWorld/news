US Midterms 2026: Democrats Flip Texas, Hold Georgia and Win Ohio Governor

Election day was 3 November 2026. By 4 November, media projections showed a Democratic night led by a first statewide Texas win since 1994 — but projections are not certified results, and several races were still unsettled or conflicting between trackers.

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 4, 2026 • 6 min read • World
01 Texas

Democrat James Talarico beat Republican Ken Paxton, 59.2% to 39.2% at 67% counted — first Democrat statewide in Texas since 1994.

02 Georgia

Incumbent Democrat Jon Ossoff won re-election, 58.9% to 41.1% at 92% counted.

03 Ohio

Democrat Amy Acton won the governorship, beating Vivek Ramaswamy — the first woman elected Ohio governor.

04 Control

Media projections later on 4 Nov put Democrats in control of both chambers; earlier trackers had not yet called the House. Treat as projected, not certified.

Midterm elections are supposed to be a referendum on the president in office, and the 2026 vote, held on 3 November, was fought on that understanding. By the morning of 4 November, the story coming out of the United States was not a close, mixed result but a Democratic night with a headline that would have seemed unlikely a decade ago: a Democrat winning statewide in Texas. The fuller picture, though, is more careful than the headline. Results reported here are media projections based on partial counts, not final certified results, and on at least one race — Maine — trackers disagreed. That distinction, between projected and certified, is the one to keep in mind throughout this article.

Texas: a first since 1994

State Representative James Talarico, a Democrat, defeated Republican Ken Paxton, the Trump-backed Texas Attorney General, for a United States Senate seat. AP figures carried by live trackers put Talarico at 59.2 percent against Paxton at 39.2 percent, with 67 percent of the expected vote counted at the time of reporting.

The historical weight of that result is why it led coverage. Talarico became the first Democrat to win statewide in Texas since 1994, and the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Texas since 1988. Texas has been the largest Republican stronghold in presidential politics for a generation; a statewide Democratic win there, even in a midterm shaped by national mood, is not a routine swing. It is the kind of result that changes how both parties think about the map, and it will be cited in every Texas campaign discussion for the next cycle — whether or not it turns out to be repeatable.

One caution applies, and it applies to every percentage in this article: these are figures at a partial count, and they will move as counting completes. The winner in Texas is projected, not certified, and the margin should be read as an indication at that stage of counting, not as a final certified margin.

Georgia: a hold that mattered

In Georgia, incumbent Democrat Jon Ossoff won re-election to a second Senate term, defeating Republican Representative Mike Collins. AP figures put Ossoff at 58.9 percent against Collins at 41.1 percent, with 92 percent counted — a more complete count than in Texas, and a clearer margin.

A hold is less dramatic than a flip, but in a Senate where Democrats needed a net gain of four seats for control, starting from a Republican 53–47 majority, every hold was a precondition for every flip. Georgia, a state that has become genuinely competitive in recent cycles, returning its Democratic incumbent by a wide projected margin, told the same story as Texas from the other direction: the night was not about one surprise, but about a pattern.

Ohio: a governorship, not a Senate seat

In Ohio, Democrat Amy Acton defeated Republican Vivek Ramaswamy for governor — not, as some early summaries implied, for the Senate. AP figures put Acton at 50.9 percent against Ramaswamy at 45.8 percent, with 87 percent counted. Acton became the first woman elected governor of Ohio, and the first Democrat elected to the office since 2006.

The distinction between governor and senator matters for what the result means. A governorship controls state administration, vetoes and appointments in Ohio; it does not change the Senate arithmetic. Conflating the two would overstate the Senate story, and it is worth being precise here because the Senate story is dramatic enough on its own.

Other Senate flips projected by AP included Roy Cooper in North Carolina, Sherrod Brown in the Ohio special election, Annie Andrews in South Carolina, Josh Turek in Iowa and Adam Hamilton in Kansas. Each of those, like Texas and Georgia, should be read as projected at the time of reporting, with final margins to be confirmed by certification.

Control: projected, and the timeline matters

Republicans entered the election with a 53–47 Senate majority; Democrats needed a net gain of four seats for control. A later update on 4 November, from Nikkei at 15:51 JST, stated that Democrats had won control of both the Senate and the House according to media projections, with AP calling the House after Democrats passed 218 seats and Senate flips clearing the 51-seat threshold.

But an earlier snapshot told a different, and instructive, story. The Indian Express live tracker at 10:40 IST on 4 November said Democrats had reached 51 Senate seats and that NBC projected the House at around 248 seats, but that AP had not yet called the House at that time. The lesson is not that one tracker was wrong; it is that “control” on election night is a rolling projection, called at different times by different outlets, and a claim that is true at 15:51 JST may not have been true at 10:40 IST. This article therefore reports control as projected by media outlets, not as an official certified outcome.

That discipline matters beyond pedantry. In the United States, the formal outcome is certification by state authorities, which follows counting, including postal and provisional ballots, and any recounts. Media calls are usually right, but they are predictions based on counted votes and statistical models — and in close races, they can be withdrawn. A reader who understands that will not be confused if a later certified margin differs from an early projected one.

What was not settled

Several races were listed as not called or too early to call in AP and Indian Express trackers, including Senate races in Michigan, Alaska, Mississippi, Minnesota, Nebraska, Idaho, Louisiana and Montana. In Maine, trackers conflicted: Nikkei and USA Today reported a win for Troy Jackson over Susan Collins, while the Indian Express tracker at 10:40 IST listed Maine as not called, with Collins leading 50.2 percent to 49.8 percent at 41 percent counted. This article does not resolve that conflict; it reports it as unsettled.

Similarly, a reported Texas governorship win for Gina Hinojosa was an NBC projection only, not an AP call in the tracker checked. Including it as a confirmed result would have been a mistake, and it is left out of the confirmed results here for that reason.

What comes next is the ordinary, unglamorous part of American elections: counting completes, states certify, and the new Congress takes its seats. The political consequences — for the remainder of the presidential term, for legislation, and for the next presidential cycle — will be argued over for months. The results themselves, once certified, will be the foundation for that argument, which is why getting the foundation right, race by race and with the right caveats, matters more than getting the headline out first.

Quick Answers
Who won the Texas Senate race in 2026? +

Democrat James Talarico defeated Republican Ken Paxton, projected at 59.2% to 39.2% at 67% counted — the first Democrat statewide win in Texas since 1994.

Did Democrats win control of Congress? +

Media projections later on 4 November put Democrats in control of both the Senate and the House. That is a projection, not a final certified result.

Did Amy Acton win a Senate seat? +

No. Amy Acton won the Ohio governorship, defeating Vivek Ramaswamy. She is the first woman elected governor of Ohio.
