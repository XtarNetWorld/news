AI's $4 Trillion Week: Nasdaq Wipes Out Its 2026 Gains in One Brutal Sell-Off

The biggest IPO in history couldn't save the market. In one week, AI stocks lost more than $4 trillion — and the poster child of the boom is now down almost half from its peak. What broke, and what comes next?

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 21, 2026 • 6 min read • Business
01 The fall

The Nasdaq fell 5.3% on Friday — 1,261.47 points to 22,617.73 — and is down 10% for the week, its worst week since March 2020. It is now negative for 2026.

Two days ago, the biggest IPO in history was a celebration. On Friday, the market took it all back — and then some. The Nasdaq fell 5.3% in a single day, AI stocks lost more than $4 trillion in a week, and the poster child of the boom is now down almost half from its peak. The same AI build-out that the world was cheering on Thursday is the thing investors were running from on Friday. What broke — and what comes next?

Friday: the day the market broke

Friday 20 November was the day the sell-off stopped being a dip and became a rout. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.3% in a single session — 1,261.47 points, down to 22,617.73 — its second-largest one-day points drop ever. For the week, the index is down 10%, its worst week since March 2020, and it has now wiped out all of its gains for 2026. The index that spent the year as the symbol of the AI boom closed the week in the red for the year.

It was not a Nasdaq-only story. The S&P 500 fell 3.7% on Friday — 265.41 points to 6,910.42 — its seventh straight daily drop, the longest losing streak since February 2020. Both the S&P and the Dow are now more than 10% below their record highs, which is the market’s formal definition of a correction. All 11 S&P sectors fell more than 1% in the week; there was nowhere to hide, not even in the defensive corners investors usually run to. The VIX, Wall Street’s fear gauge, soared 33% to 37.

The scale of the money involved is hard to hold in your head, so here it is plainly: Nasdaq members lost more than $4 trillion in market value in a single week, according to the Wall Street Journal. That is not a bad day. That is a week that erased, in five trading sessions, more value than most countries’ entire stock markets are worth.

Nvidia: the poster child falls hardest

No stock tells the story of this week better than Nvidia. It fell 14% on Friday alone, 23% for the week, and is now about 48% below its early-October peak. In 45 days, $2.8 trillion of its market value has gone. The company that was, weeks ago, the most valuable in the world and the face of the AI build-out is now the face of its unwind.

Two company-specific blows landed on top of the market-wide selling. Jensen Huang, the chief executive who built Nvidia into the AI era’s central company, has resigned amid scandal. And Reuters has reported an SEC accounting probe into the company. Either event alone would have hurt the stock; together, in a week when investors were already questioning whether AI spending could ever pay for itself, they turned a correction into a collapse.

It is worth saying plainly what this does and does not mean. A 48% fall from a peak is not the same as a broken business — Nvidia still sells the chips the AI industry runs on. But it does mean the market has stopped pricing the company as if its growth could only go in one direction. The debate is no longer whether AI is real. It is whether the money being poured into it will ever come back.

Anthropic: the IPO that could not save the week

Two days before the rout, the story was the opposite. Anthropic’s debut on Thursday was the biggest IPO in history, closing 17.1% above its $83 offer price — the kind of opening that usually sets the tone for a market. On Friday, Anthropic fell 8.5–9.1% to about $88.91. It is still above its IPO price, and still about 23% below its $115.10 intraday peak from the day before — but the celebration lasted barely a day before the market’s mood swallowed it.

That whiplash is the story of this week in miniature. The IPO was real, the demand was real, and the price still holds above where it started. But a single listing, however large, cannot hold up a market that has decided to reprice an entire trade at once. The money that cheered Anthropic on Thursday was the same money selling Nvidia on Friday.

One caution on the numbers: secondary briefings have carried conflicting totals for the IPO’s proceeds and market capitalisation. This article does not use them. The price moves above — the $83 offer, the $115.10 peak, the $88.91 close — are consistent across the sources cited, and they are the figures that matter.

The turn: from celebration to correction

What makes this week disorienting is how fast the mood turned. Two days ago, the site’s own coverage of the Anthropic IPO was a story about the AI build-out reaching its biggest moment yet. The same build-out — the same chips, the same data centres, the same spending — is now the thing investors are fleeing. Nothing about the technology changed in 48 hours. What changed was the price investors were willing to pay for the future.

That is how corrections work: they rarely arrive with a single cause. A resignation here, a probe there, a bad week of trading, and suddenly the questions that were easy to ignore — how much is being spent, when does it pay back, what if growth slows — are the only questions anyone is asking. The Nasdaq’s worst week since March 2020 did not happen because one thing went wrong. It happened because, all at once, the market stopped being sure.

What happens next

Three things will decide how this week is remembered. The first is Monday: whether buyers step in at these lower prices or the selling feeds on itself for another day. The second is what the SEC probe into Nvidia actually finds — and whether more follows it. The third is the spending question itself: whether the companies pouring hundreds of billions into AI can show, in the next round of results, that the money is coming back.

Until then, the honest summary is the numbers. Nasdaq down 10% in a week. More than $4 trillion gone. Nvidia down almost half from its peak. A fear gauge at 37. And an IPO that was the biggest in history, still above its offer price — but no longer the story. The story, for now, is the sell-off.
