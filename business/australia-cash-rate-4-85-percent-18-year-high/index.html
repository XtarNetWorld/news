Australia Lifts Rates to 4.85%: A Split Vote at an 18-Year High

On 3 November 2026, Australia’s central bank raised borrowing costs for the fifth time this year, even as unemployment rose and house prices fell. The split vote is the real signal.

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 3, 2026 • 6 min read • Business
01 The Decision

Australia’s cash rate rose 25 basis points to 4.85% on 3 November 2026, its fifth increase this year.

02 The Split

The vote was 6–3. Three board members wanted to leave the rate unchanged at 4.60%.

03 The Reason

Underlying inflation reached 3.7% in the September quarter, above the bank’s 2–3% target range.

04 The Cost

Unemployment was 4.7% in September, a five-year high, and house prices are falling. The trade-off is now explicit.

Australia has just raised interest rates to a level it has not seen since 2008, and it did so in a divided vote. That combination — a firm move, with a third of the board against it — tells you more about the economy than the headline rate alone.

What happened

On 3 November 2026, the Reserve Bank of Australia’s Monetary Policy Board lifted the cash rate target by 25 basis points, from 4.60% to 4.85%. It was the fifth increase of 2026, and it took total tightening this year to 125 basis points, more than reversing 75 basis points of easing in 2025.

The decision was not unanimous. Six members voted to raise the rate, and three voted to leave it unchanged at 4.60%. In its statement, the board said further tightening was warranted to support the return of inflation to target, given the revised inflation outlook and continuing upside risks. It also said it would continue to do what it considers necessary to bring inflation sustainably back to target.

One change of wording mattered to markets: the board removed a reference to increasing the cash rate further if needed. After the decision, markets were wagering on a December hold.

Why it matters

The cash rate is the anchor for much of Australia’s borrowing costs. When it rises, variable home-loan rates, business loans and other forms of credit tend to become more expensive, although lenders are not required to pass on the full change, and they may move at different times and by different amounts.

That is why this decision lands unevenly. For savers, higher rates can improve returns on deposits. For borrowers, the same move raises repayments and can reduce how much they can borrow. For businesses, it raises the cost of financing investment and working capital at the same time as demand is softening.

The increase comes after a year in which borrowing costs have already risen sharply. The central bank is, in effect, accepting more near-term pain in the hope of avoiding a longer period of high inflation.

The numbers behind the decision

The inflation data released before the meeting left little room for a pause in the majority’s view. Headline consumer prices rose 4.3% in the 12 months to September, up from 4.0% in August. Trimmed mean inflation — the quarterly measure the bank weighs most heavily because it strips out the largest price swings — rose 3.7% over the year to the September quarter.

Both measures sit above the bank’s 2–3% target range. The bank’s updated forecasts now expect inflation to remain above 3% for the coming year and not to reach the top of the target range until late 2027, around three quarters later than previously forecast.

The trade-offs, in plain terms

On one side of the argument is inflation itself. When price rises stay high for a long time, people and businesses start to expect them to continue. That can make inflation harder to bring down later, because wage and price decisions begin to build it in. The majority’s case is that acting now, even painfully, reduces that risk.

On the other side is the economy’s current weakness. Unemployment has risen to a five-year high. House prices are falling at their fastest pace in more than 40 years, according to reporting of the decision, which can curb household spending through weaker confidence and lower housing turnover. The dissenting members, in the account given after the meeting, placed greater weight on those downside risks.

  • For borrowers: check what your lender actually announces, and when it takes effect, rather than assuming the full 25 basis points is passed on immediately.
  • For buyers: higher rates reduce borrowing capacity, so a budget based on earlier rates may no longer hold.
  • For businesses: the cost of new borrowing has risen, but the bank’s message is that demand will need to remain subdued for inflation to fall.
  • For savers: deposit rates may improve, but that benefit should be weighed against higher living costs while inflation remains above target.

What happens next

The next decision is due on 8 December 2026, the bank’s final meeting of the year. Before then, three sets of information will matter most: the minutes of the November meeting, due on 17 November; October jobs figures, due on 19 November; and monthly consumer prices for October, due on 25 November.

Forecasts from Australia’s major banks are split, but lean towards a pause. Three of the four major banks do not expect another hike in December, while ANZ flags a risk that the cash rate reaches 5.10% in December or February. If a sixth increase did occur, it would take the rate to a level not seen since 2008.

The broader question is whether November proves to be the peak of this tightening cycle. A split vote, a change in wording, and market bets on a December hold all point in that direction. But the bank has deliberately left its next move open, and another inflation surprise could change the picture quickly. For now, the safest reading is the one the vote itself suggests: the bank is willing to raise rates again, but it is no longer sure it needs to.

Quick Answers
What is Australia's cash rate now? +

It is 4.85%, after the Reserve Bank of Australia raised it by 25 basis points on 3 November 2026. That was the fifth increase of 2026 and the highest level since 2008.

Why did the Reserve Bank raise rates if unemployment is rising? +

Because inflation, on the measures the bank weighs most heavily, is still above its 2-3% target range and broadly based. The board judged that the risk of higher inflation expectations becoming embedded outweighed, for now, the pain from slower growth, weaker housing and a softer labour market.

Will Australian interest rates rise again in December? +

That is not settled. The next decision is due on 8 December 2026. Three of Australia's four major banks do not expect another hike in December, while ANZ flags a risk of a rise to 5.10% in December or February, and markets after the split vote were wagering on a hold.
