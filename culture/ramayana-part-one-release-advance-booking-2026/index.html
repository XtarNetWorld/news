Ramayana Part One Releases 5 November: Advance Bookings, Cast and the Pushpak Row

Nitesh Tiwari’s two-part retelling of Valmiki’s epic opens worldwide on 5 November 2026, after advance bookings that trade trackers put above Rs 96 crore including blocked seats — and a promotional remark that turned into a debate of its own.

MS By Monu Sharma
• November 4, 2026 • 7 min read • Culture
01 Release

5 November 2026 worldwide, after the makers clarified the date in October. Part Two is planned for Diwali 2027.

02 Advance

India advance bookings of Rs 96.56 crore including blocked seats, Rs 77.31 crore without, per Sacnilk via NDTV Profit on 4 November.

03 Cast

Ranbir Kapoor as Rama, Sai Pallavi as Sita, Yash as Ravana and Sunny Deol as Hanuman.

04 The row

Ranbir Kapoor’s Pushpak Viman remark — “India did it 5,000 years ago” — went viral during promotions.

Few Indian films arrive with this much riding on them before a single ticket for opening day has been torn. Ramayana Part One is not just another big-budget release: it is the first half of a planned two-part retelling of Valmiki’s epic, directed by Nitesh Tiwari, made with the visual-effects scale of Prime Focus Studios and DNEG, and released into a Diwali-season audience that has already been buying tickets for weeks. The question on 5 November is no longer whether people are curious — advance bookings have answered that — but whether the film itself can carry the weight of the epic, the budget and the debate around it.

What is releasing, and when

Ramayana Part One releases worldwide on 5 November 2026. The date itself needed clarifying: an international trailer from Sony Pictures had suggested 6 November, but the makers settled the confusion in October, confirming 5 November across premium formats, with a new poster featuring Ranbir Kapoor and Sai Pallavi. Advance bookings opened on 20 October 2026, on Dussehra — more than two weeks before release, which is itself a signal of how the makers expected demand to build.

The film is directed by Nitesh Tiwari, with the screenplay co-written with Shridhar Raghavan. It is produced by Namit Malhotra’s Prime Focus Studios and DNEG, in association with Yash’s Monster Mind Creations. That combination matters because it pairs a director known for emotionally grounded mainstream films with a visual-effects house that has worked at international scale — the bet, in other words, is that this Ramayana will feel both familiar and unlike anything Indian audiences have seen on screen before.

Part One premiered at the Academy Museum of Motion Pictures in Los Angeles on 27 October 2026, ahead of its commercial release. A premiere in Los Angeles does not guarantee a reception in India, but it does show where the makers are pitching the film: not only as a domestic Diwali event, but as an Indian epic presented to a global audience.

Who plays whom

Ranbir Kapoor plays Rama and Sai Pallavi plays Sita. Yash, best known to Hindi audiences from the KGF films, plays Ravana, and Sunny Deol plays Hanuman. Ravi Dubey plays Lakshmana. The supporting cast reported includes Arun Govil as Dasharatha, Lara Dutta as Kaikeyi and Rakul Preet Singh as Shurpanakha.

Casting is where a retelling like this is won or lost before release, and the choices here are deliberately cross-industry: a Hindi-film lead, a performer from Malayalam and Tamil cinema as Sita, a Kannada star as Ravana, and a veteran Hindi actor as Hanuman. The idea is a pan-Indian cast for a pan-Indian story — but it also means every performance will be judged against both the epic as audiences know it and the actors’ own previous work, which is a harder test than a single-industry cast would face.

The advance-booking numbers, explained

NDTV Profit reported on 4 November 2026, citing trade tracker Sacnilk, that India advance bookings stood at Rs 96.56 crore including blocked seats, and Rs 77.31 crore without blocked seats, with 25.65 lakh tickets sold. Day 1 alone accounted for Rs 39.35 crore from 10.11 lakh tickets across 23,671 shows, with Hindi contributing Rs 78.88 crore, about 82 percent of the total.

Those numbers need two cautions, and both matter for anyone reading headlines on release day. First, “including blocked seats” counts seats held by theatres and distributors, not only tickets a member of the public has bought — the without-blocked-seats figure, Rs 77.31 crore, is the cleaner measure of audience demand. Second, advance bookings are not box-office collections. A film can open on pre-sales and still depend on word of mouth from Friday onwards; the advance figure tells you about curiosity and distribution reach, not about whether audiences liked what they saw.

What the advance figure does establish is scale. More than 23,000 shows on day one, and a Hindi share above 80 percent, point to a release that is being treated as a national event rather than a regional one — even though the cast and the story are deliberately pan-Indian. The remaining question is how the other language versions perform once the film is actually playing.

The Pushpak Viman remark — what was actually said

During promotions, Ranbir Kapoor said, in a promotional interview with presenter Suma on her Chat Show, joined by co-stars Ravi Dubey, Rakul Preet Singh and Yash and director Nitesh Tiwari: “Elon Musk recently invented a flying car. I think India did it 5,000 years ago!” He was referring to the Pushpaka Vimana, the divine flying chariot of the Ramayana.

A clip of the interview circulated on social media and drew both amusement and criticism, with users questioning a comparison between mythology and modern engineering. Reports also noted that it followed a Tesla Roadster demonstration by Elon Musk in Texas on 15 October 2026. In the same interview, Tiwari said it took close to two years to arrive at the final Pushpaka Vimana design for the film — a detail that is easy to lose in the argument, but which says something about how seriously the production treated the epic’s fantastical elements.

The useful distinction here is one the film itself will have to make: the Pushpaka Vimana is a mythological and literary concept, not a documented technological invention. Treating it as the latter turns a story about imagination into a claim about history, and that is where the remark drew pushback. As a piece of promotion, though, it did what promotion is meant to do — it got people talking about the film, its world and its ideas, days before release.

What to watch after release

Three things will decide whether Ramayana Part One is remembered as an event or as a landmark. The first is the opening weekend itself — not the advance figure, but how collections hold from Friday to Sunday once audiences, not just fans, have seen it. The second is whether the film works for viewers who know the epic intimately and for viewers meeting it fresh; a retelling that only pleases one group will struggle to justify a second part. The third is Part Two, already planned for Diwali 2027: a two-part structure only pays off if the first part leaves audiences wanting the second, rather than feeling the story has been stretched.

None of that can be judged from trailers, advance bookings or a viral clip. What can be said now is that the film arrives with an unusually clear set of ingredients — a known epic, a pan-Indian cast, a director with a mainstream track record, and pre-release demand that is already among the strongest of the year. On 5 November, the audience gets to decide whether those ingredients became a film.
